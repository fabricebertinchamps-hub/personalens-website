Can ChatGPT analyse your text messages?

Yes. You can paste or upload a WhatsApp or iMessage conversation into ChatGPT and it will summarise it, describe the tone, and offer opinions on what each person seems to feel. What it will not do on its own is tell you how confident it is, quote the evidence behind each claim, stay consistent if you ask twice, or resist telling you what you hoped to hear. Those gaps matter far more for a relationship question than for a work email.

The short answer

ChatGPT can analyse text messages, and for some jobs it is genuinely good: summarising a long thread, spotting when the tone shifted, rephrasing a reply you are nervous about sending. Where it struggles is the job most people actually bring to it, which is a judgement about a person: is he losing interest, is my mother manipulative, is this friendship one-sided. That kind of judgement needs a stable method, a sense of how much evidence is enough, and a willingness to disappoint you. A general-purpose chatbot has none of those by default.

None of this is a reason not to use it. It is a reason to use it knowing where the edges are.

What ChatGPT does well with a chat

Summarising long threads

Give it three weeks of messages and ask "what were the main disagreements?" and you will usually get a fair list. Large language models are very good at compressing text, and a chat is just text.

Describing tone in a single message

"Does this sound cold?" is a question it handles reasonably well, because the answer depends on wording that is right there on the page. It will pick up hedges ("maybe", "I guess"), clipped replies, and the difference between "ok" and "okay!".

Drafting replies

If you know what you want to say and cannot find the words, it is a useful editor. This is where most people get real value.

Inferring broad personality signals

There is real research here. Peters and Matz (2024, PNAS Nexus) found that GPT models could infer Big Five traits from people's social media posts with no special training, reaching correlations in the same modest range as older, purpose-built language models. That builds on a decade of work showing that word choice carries personality information, from Pennebaker and King (1999) to Schwartz et al. (2013) and Park et al. (2015). So the underlying idea, that language reveals something real about the writer, is sound.

Five places it quietly fails

1. It leans towards agreeing with you

Language models trained on human feedback tend to tell people what they appear to want to hear. Researchers at Anthropic documented this "sycophancy" across several assistants (Sharma et al., 2023). In a relationship question the effect is easy to trigger by accident. "Is my boyfriend being distant?" invites a yes. "Am I overthinking this?" invites a different yes. Ask both on the same chat and you can get two confident, opposite readings.

Test it yourself: paste the same thread twice in fresh conversations, once framed from your side and once framed neutrally ("describe the communication pattern of both people"). If the conclusions flip, you are reading your own framing back.

2. It does not know what is normal

A reply time of four hours means nothing without a baseline. Is that slow for this person? Slow compared to three months ago? A chatbot reading one pasted excerpt has no base rate, so it fills the gap with general assumptions. Our guide to what reply time really says covers why the change in someone's pattern matters more than the absolute number. ChatGPT cannot see change unless you give it the before and the after, and tell it to compare.

3. Long chats get skimmed in the middle

Models handle very long inputs less evenly than people assume. Liu et al. (2024, Transactions of the ACL) showed that language models use information at the start and end of a long context better than information buried in the middle, the "lost in the middle" effect. A year of WhatsApp history is exactly the kind of input where the important stretch might be in month seven. Ask about something specific from the middle of the chat and check whether it actually finds it.

4. It is not consistent between runs

Ask the same question about the same chat tomorrow and the wording, the emphasis and sometimes the verdict will change. That is fine for brainstorming. It is not fine when you are trying to decide whether a pattern is real, because a stable measure is the minimum requirement for trusting any measure. Psychologists call this reliability, and it comes before validity: a scale that gives you a different weight every time cannot be accurate.

5. It rarely shows its evidence

"She seems emotionally avoidant" is a claim. "She changes the subject in 6 of the 8 threads where you mention the future; here are the lines" is evidence. Unless you explicitly ask, a chatbot gives you the first kind. Claims without quoted evidence are hard to check and easy to over-believe, especially at 1am.

How to get a more honest reading from ChatGPT

If you are going to use a general chatbot, a few habits make a big difference:

  • Strip names and identifiers first. Replace names with A and B, remove phone numbers, addresses and anything you would not want stored. Check your data settings too: consumer ChatGPT conversations may be used to improve models unless you turn that off in the data controls.
  • Ask about both people. "Describe how A and B each handle conflict in this chat" produces a fairer reading than "what's wrong with B".
  • Demand quotes. "For every claim, quote the message that supports it. If there is no supporting message, say so."
  • Ask for the counter-case. "Now give the most charitable reading of B's behaviour." If the charitable reading is as convincing as the first one, the chat may simply not contain enough to decide.
  • Ask how much evidence there is. "Is this enough text to say anything reliable about personality?" A good answer will often be no. See our guide on how many messages you need.
  • Run it twice. Only trust conclusions that survive a fresh conversation.

A prompt you can copy

"Below is a chat between A and B. Do not take sides. (1) Describe each person's communication pattern: who initiates, who repairs after conflict, how each handles disagreement. (2) For each observation, quote the supporting lines. (3) Say which conclusions are well supported and which are guesses. (4) Give the most charitable interpretation of each person. (5) Tell me what this chat cannot tell me."

This will not fix inconsistency between runs, and it will not give the model a baseline it does not have. It will make the output more checkable, which is most of the battle.

What a purpose-built tool adds

The difference between a general chatbot and a dedicated analysis tool is not mainly the model. It is everything around the model: a fixed method applied the same way every time, a frame chosen for the relationship in question, a stated confidence level based on how much text there is, and evidence quoted by default rather than on request.

When not to use any AI for this

If you are trying to work out whether you are safe, a chatbot is the wrong tool and so is any app. Patterns like threats, monitoring your phone, controlling money, or isolating you from friends need a human who knows your situation. Our guide to emotional abuse patterns includes where to start. And if the question is your own distress rather than someone else's behaviour, talk to a qualified professional.

Frequently asked questions

Can ChatGPT read a WhatsApp chat export?

Yes. You can upload the .txt file from a WhatsApp export or paste the text directly. Very long chats may be truncated or unevenly read, so for a specific question it often works better to paste the relevant stretch of conversation.

Is ChatGPT accurate at analysing relationships from texts?

It is good at summarising and describing tone, but it has no baseline for what is normal for the people involved, tends to agree with how you frame the question, and can give different verdicts on different runs. Treat its conclusions as hypotheses and ask it to quote the supporting messages.

Is it safe to paste my private messages into ChatGPT?

Remove names, numbers and identifying details first, and check your data controls, because consumer conversations may be used to improve models unless you opt out. Remember the other person in the chat has not consented, so share only what you need.

What is the best prompt to analyse a text conversation?

Ask the model not to take sides, to describe both people's patterns, to quote a message for every claim, to separate well-supported conclusions from guesses, and to state what the chat cannot tell you. Then run the same prompt again in a new conversation and keep only what survives both runs.
