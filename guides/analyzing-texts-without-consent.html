Is it ethical to analyse someone's texts without them knowing?

If you were part of the conversation, reflecting on it privately, even with help from a tool, is broadly the same as rereading it or showing it to a trusted friend, and most people would accept it. It becomes unethical when you analyse messages you were never meant to see, when the purpose is control or ammunition rather than understanding, or when the result is shared beyond you. The questions below help you work out which side of that line you are on.

Two very different things get called "analysing someone's texts"

Most of the confusion around this question comes from lumping together two acts that are ethically far apart.

Reflecting on a conversation you were in. You received those messages. They were written to you. Rereading them, thinking about them, asking a friend "what do you make of this?", or using a tool to see patterns is an extension of what people have always done with letters and conversations. The other person cannot reasonably expect you to forget what they told you.

Accessing messages you were never part of. Going through a partner's phone, reading their chats with someone else, installing an app to see their WhatsApp, logging into their accounts. This is surveillance. It breaks a clear expectation of privacy, is often illegal, and in relationships it is closely associated with control. Researchers studying technology-enabled intimate partner abuse have documented how often phone access and monitoring apps are part of that pattern (Freed et al., 2018).

Nothing in this guide makes the second act acceptable, and no analysis tool should be used for it. The rest of the guide is about the first, which is where the real grey areas are.

Why it still feels uncomfortable

Even when you were in the conversation, there is a reason the question nags. The philosopher Helen Nissenbaum's idea of contextual integrity (2004) captures it well: information is shared under the norms of a context. A message sent to you at midnight was shared for you to read, in that moment, as part of your relationship. It was not shared to be fed into software, scored, or summarised for a third party.

So the ethical question is not "did they consent to this specific tool?" They almost never will have. It is "how far does what I am doing move their words from the context they were shared in, and is that move justified?"

A useful comparison: showing a message to your closest friend and asking what they think is a small move most people accept. Posting the same message publicly to ask strangers is a large move most people do not. Private, temporary, tool-assisted reflection sits much closer to the first.

Six questions to ask yourself first

1. Was I part of this conversation?

If no, stop. Messages between other people are not yours to analyse, however worried or suspicious you are.

2. What am I actually trying to find out?

"Why do our arguments always go the same way?" and "Am I the one escalating?" are questions about understanding. "How can I prove she's lying?" or "What can I use against him?" are questions about winning. Analysis in service of understanding is easier to justify than analysis in service of ammunition. If you notice you want a verdict to send them, pause.

3. Where will the words and the result go?

Kept to yourself, or discussed with a therapist, is one thing. Screenshots in a group chat, a post on social media, or sending them "the AI says you're avoidant" is another. The more widely the result travels, the more it needs their agreement.

4. Will the tool keep their words?

A service that processes the chat and discards it moves their words less far than one that stores it indefinitely or trains on it. Check before you upload. Our upload safety checklist covers what to look for.

5. Could I tell them I did this?

Not "would they be thrilled" but "could I explain it honestly without embarrassment?" "I was confused about our last few fights, so I reread our messages and used an app to help me see patterns" is explainable. If the honest description makes you wince, that is information.

6. Am I using only what I need?

If your question is about the last month, you do not need five years. If a thread contains their health problems or a friend's secret that has nothing to do with your question, cut it out before uploading.

Remember they cannot answer back

There is a second ethical point that is less about privacy and more about fairness. A reading of a conversation is one-sided by design. The other person cannot explain what they meant, add context you do not have, or point out what they were dealing with that week. Any interpretation, from a friend or from software, should be held with that in mind.

Practically, this means:

  • Treat the result as a hypothesis, not a verdict. "This suggests she withdraws when I raise the future" is something to explore with her, not a fact about her.
  • Read yourself as carefully as them. Most conflict is a loop two people make together. Our guide how to tell if you're the toxic one exists because the most useful finding is often about your own side.
  • Do not diagnose. No chat analysis can tell you someone has a personality disorder. Labels like "narcissist" said to someone's face, based on a reading of their texts, do far more harm than good.

When analysing the chat is clearly justified

Some situations tip the balance firmly. If you are being belittled, threatened, or worn down by someone's messages, keeping and reviewing those messages is not a privacy violation. It is self-protection. Our guide to gaslighting in text messages recommends keeping records precisely because written messages are evidence of a pattern that is hard to hold onto in the moment.

If you think you may be in an abusive relationship, a domestic abuse service can help you think about safety, including how to store evidence safely. Be careful with any tool on a shared or monitored device. If you are in immediate danger, contact local emergency services.

What about the law?

Laws differ by country, so this is not legal advice. Broadly: accessing someone else's phone or accounts without permission can be a criminal offence in many places, and installing monitoring software on another adult's device often is. Keeping and rereading messages someone sent you is generally treated very differently. In the EU, the GDPR does not apply to processing carried out by a person in the course of a purely personal or household activity, though that exemption does not cover publishing someone's messages. Sharing private messages publicly can raise separate legal issues, such as privacy or defamation claims, depending on where you live.

Should you tell them?

You do not owe anyone a report every time you think about a conversation. But if the reading changes how you see the relationship, the honest next step is usually to talk to them about the pattern, not the tool. "I've noticed we tend to stop talking for days after an argument, and I don't want that" opens a conversation. "An app says you stonewall me" closes one.

In some relationships, especially with colleagues, it may also be worth agreeing norms openly. A team that agrees to look at its own group chat together, as part of improving how it works, is doing something very different from one member analysing the others in secret. Our Group lens works best in that open spirit.

Our position

We build a tool that reads conversations, so we have a stake in this answer, and it is fair to say so. Our view is that understanding a conversation you were part of is legitimate, that the other person's words deserve care, and that the tool should be designed to respect both: no account, no retention of the chat after the reading, no training on it, and readings that quote the evidence so you can judge it yourself rather than accept a label. We will not support reading chats you were not part of, and we discourage using readings as weapons.

This guide is general information, not legal or clinical advice. If a relationship feels unsafe, please reach out to a qualified professional or a local support service.

Frequently asked questions

Is it ethical to analyse a conversation I was part of?

Generally yes, if you keep it private, use it to understand the relationship rather than to build ammunition, avoid services that store or train on the chat, and treat the result as a hypothesis rather than a verdict about the other person.

Is it okay to read my partner's messages to someone else?

No. Accessing messages you were not part of is surveillance, breaks a clear expectation of privacy, is often illegal, and is a recognised feature of controlling relationships. If you are worried about the relationship, talk to your partner or a counsellor instead.

Do I need the other person's consent to use an AI tool on our chat?

For private reflection on a conversation you were in, most people do not seek consent, much as they would not before showing a message to a close friend. Consent becomes important if you plan to share the result, publish their words, or use a service that keeps the chat.

Is analysing someone's texts legal?

It depends on the country. Accessing someone else's phone or accounts without permission is illegal in many places, while rereading messages sent to you generally is not. Publishing private messages can raise separate legal issues. This is not legal advice.

Can I use chat analysis as evidence of abuse?

Keeping and reviewing abusive messages you received is a legitimate form of self-protection. An AI reading is not legal evidence in itself, but the original messages may be. A domestic abuse service can advise on storing them safely.
