Why do I text differently with different people?

You text differently with different people because language adapts to its audience. Decades of research show that people automatically adjust their wording, length and tone to the person they are talking to, and that we behave differently in different relationships because each one activates different expectations. It is not inconsistency or fakeness. It becomes worth a closer look only when the difference is driven by fear, such as rewriting every message to one person so they will not be upset.

The short version: you are supposed to

Look at your last messages to your mother, your best friend, your partner and your manager. Different lengths, different emoji, different amounts of apologising, maybe different languages. Many people find this slightly unsettling, as if one of those versions must be the "real" them and the rest a performance.

The research says otherwise. Adapting how you speak to who you are speaking to is one of the most consistent findings in the study of language and social behaviour. The question is not why you change, but how, and whether the change serves you.

Four reasons your texting style changes

1. You adjust to your audience, automatically

Communication Accommodation Theory, developed by Howard Giles and colleagues from the 1970s onwards, describes how people shift their speech towards the people they want to connect with (convergence) and away from people they want to distance themselves from (divergence). The sociolinguist Allan Bell (1984) put it even more simply: style is largely "audience design." You speak to be understood and received by a particular listener.

In texting, this shows up as matching: you start using someone's abbreviations, their emoji, their message length. If a friend sends voice notes, you are more likely to send them back.

2. Matching tracks closeness

James Pennebaker's lab measured this precisely. Niederhoffer and Pennebaker (2002) found that people in conversation converge on small, almost invisible function words (pronouns, articles, prepositions) without realising. Ireland and colleagues (2011, Psychological Science) then showed that this "language style matching" predicted which speed-dating pairs wanted to meet again, and which couples were still together months later, based on their instant messages.

So part of why you text differently with different people is that you are more in sync with some than others. The close friend whose style you have absorbed over years is not getting a fake you. They are getting a shared one.

3. Power changes who adapts to whom

Accommodation is not symmetrical. In a study of Wikipedia editors and US Supreme Court transcripts, Danescu-Niculescu-Mizil and colleagues (2012) found that people coordinate their language more towards those with more power than the other way round. That is why your messages to your boss are more careful, more formal and more likely to mirror their style than theirs are to mirror yours. Our guide on reading your boss from their messages uses this asymmetry as a signal.

4. Each relationship activates a different version of you

Personality is not a fixed setting that plays identically everywhere. Walter Mischel and Yuichi Shoda (1995) argued that consistency lives in stable if-then patterns: if criticised by a parent, then defensive; if teased by a friend, then playful. William Fleeson (2001) showed that people's moment-to-moment behaviour varies widely around their average traits.

Attachment works the same way. Baldwin and colleagues (1996) found that people hold several relationship models, not one, and different relationships call up different ones. Fraley and colleagues (2011) developed a questionnaire precisely because attachment security differs across relationships with a mother, a father, a partner and a best friend. You can be relaxed and secure with your sister and anxious with your partner, and your texts will show it. Our guide to attachment style from texts explains the markers.

What actually changes: a quick audit

Pick three chats: one with family, one with a friend, one with a partner or colleague. Scroll back a few weeks in each and notice:

  • Length. Do you write paragraphs to one person and fragments to another?
  • Who starts. Are you the initiator in some chats and the responder in others?
  • Hedging and apologising. "Sorry to bother you", "just wondering", "no worries if not": where do these cluster?
  • Emoji and punctuation. Where do the exclamation marks appear, and where do they disappear?
  • Speed. Who gets an instant reply, and who waits until you have the energy?
  • Language. If you are multilingual, which language do you use with whom, and when do you switch?
  • Inherited phrases. Some of what you write with family may not be yours at all. See inherited language.

The interesting results are usually not "I'm more formal with my boss," which you already knew. They are the surprises: that you apologise constantly to one friend, or that your messages to a parent are shorter than to anyone else.

When the difference is healthy

  • You are more formal at work and more playful with friends.
  • You have private jokes, nicknames and shorthand with people you are close to.
  • You match a grieving friend's quieter tone.
  • You switch languages or dialects between family and colleagues.
  • Your style has drifted towards your partner's over the years.

All of these are accommodation doing its job: making communication smoother and signalling connection.

When it is worth a closer look

Adapting becomes a problem when it is driven by fear rather than connection. Some signs:

  • You draft, rewrite and re-read messages to one person because you are afraid of how they will react.
  • You never disagree in writing with someone, even when you disagree in your head.
  • Your messages to one person are full of apologies for ordinary things.
  • You feel like a different, smaller person in one chat and relieved when you close it.

That pattern sometimes reflects people-pleasing, where keeping others comfortable comes at your own expense. Our guide on how to stop people-pleasing covers it. In some relationships it reflects something more serious: walking on eggshells around someone whose reactions feel unpredictable or punishing. If that describes a partner or family member, our guides on emotional abuse patterns and a controlling parent's communication may help, and talking to a qualified professional is worth considering.

Why "the same person reads differently in every chat" matters

This is also the reason any honest analysis of someone's messages has to ask which relationship it is reading. A person's texts to their mother cannot tell you how they behave with a partner, and a work Slack cannot tell you how someone handles intimacy. The family you see in the family chat is a different slice of you than the one your friends see, as our guide on why your friend group sees you differently from your family explores.

Frequently asked questions

Is it normal to text differently with different people?

Yes. People automatically adapt their language to their audience and relationship, a pattern documented in communication accommodation research and studies of language style matching. Different relationships also activate different expectations and attachment patterns.

Does texting differently mean I am fake?

No. Adapting your style is how people signal connection and make communication smoother. It becomes worth examining only if the difference is driven by fear, such as constantly rewriting messages so one person will not be upset.

Why do I start texting like my partner or friends?

People converge on the language of those they feel close to, often without noticing, down to small words like pronouns and articles. Research has found that this kind of matching in instant messages is associated with relationship stability.

Why am I more formal when texting my boss?

Power affects accommodation. Research shows people adjust their language more towards those with more power than the reverse, so messages to a manager tend to be more careful and formal.

Can my texts show a different attachment style with different people?

Yes. Attachment security varies across relationships, so you might write securely to a sibling and anxiously to a partner. That is why reading one chat cannot describe all your relationships.
