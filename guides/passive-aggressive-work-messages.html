Passive-aggressive work messages: 12 examples and how to reply

A work message is passive-aggressive when it expresses irritation indirectly, through phrasing, timing or audience, rather than saying the problem out loud. Classic examples are "per my last email", "friendly reminder" and copying your manager without warning. But text strips out tone, and people consistently read neutral work messages as more negative than they were meant, so many "passive-aggressive" messages are really just terse. The examples below show how to tell the difference and how to reply either way.

First, a warning about your own reading

Before the examples, the most important research finding in this area: we are bad at reading tone in writing, and we do not know it. Kruger, Epley, Parker and Ng (2005) found that people sending emails were confident their tone (sarcasm, seriousness, humour) would come across, and recipients were much less accurate than senders expected. Kristin Byron (2008) reviewed the evidence on workplace email and argued that recipients tend to read neutral messages as more negative than intended, and positive ones as more neutral.

So when a work message feels passive-aggressive, there are two possibilities: the sender meant it, or text did what text does. The clue is rarely one message. It is the pattern: who it comes from, how often, and whether it happens with everyone or just you.

The 12 examples

1. "Per my last email"

What it can mean: "I already answered this, and you did not read it." It is the most famous passive-aggressive phrase in office life for a reason.

When it is neutral: when it simply points to where the information is, especially from people who write formally.

How to reply: take the hint at face value and fix the gap. "Thanks, I missed that. So we're going with option B?" Answering the substance removes the sting.

2. "As previously mentioned" / "As I said"

A close cousin of the first. Repeated within a short thread, it signals growing impatience. Reply with a brief acknowledgement and a question that moves things forward, or suggest a five-minute call if the same point keeps getting lost.

3. "Friendly reminder"

The "friendly" is doing heavy lifting. From someone waiting on you for a week, it often means "this is late." From an automated tool or a project coordinator, it is just a reminder. Reply with a date: "Thanks, you'll have it by Thursday."

4. "Just following up" / "Circling back"

Usually neutral; following up is part of many jobs. It reads as passive-aggressive when the gap between follow-ups shrinks, or when it arrives with your manager newly copied. Reply with status, even if the status is "not yet."

5. "Thanks in advance"

Polite on the surface, but it assumes compliance, which is why it can feel like pressure. Most of the time, it is simply a habit. Not worth reacting to on its own.

6. "Noted." / "Fine." / "K."

A one-word reply to a long or careful message can feel like a door closing. Sometimes it is: a deliberate refusal to engage. Often it is someone on their phone between meetings. Check the baseline: does this person write one-word replies to everyone? If yes, it is style. If they normally write paragraphs to you, the change is the signal.

7. The lone thumbs-up

You send a detailed proposal. The response is a 👍 reaction and nothing else. For some people, the thumbs-up means "read, agreed, no notes." For others, especially when it lands on something that clearly needed a reply, it is a way of ending the conversation without engaging. Reactions are a newer and genuinely ambiguous signal. If you need a real answer, ask a direct question: "Great. Any changes before I send it to the client?"

8. The surprise CC

Your manager, or theirs, suddenly appears on the thread. Sometimes it is legitimate visibility. When it appears right after a disagreement, without explanation, it is an escalation dressed up as transparency. Reply calmly to everyone with facts, and raise the CC privately with the sender: "I noticed you added Sam. Is there something you're worried about that I can help with?"

9. "Not sure if you saw this"

Often a polite nudge. When it comes minutes after the original message, it means "why haven't you replied?" Reply when you can, and if quick replies are not possible in your role, say so once: "I batch messages twice a day, so I'll usually come back to you by the afternoon."

10. "Going forward, please..."

Feedback framed as a new rule, delivered in writing, sometimes in front of others. It may be a fair request delivered awkwardly. It may be a public correction. Reply with a short "Understood," and if the request itself is unclear or unfair, discuss it one-to-one.

11. "Correct me if I'm wrong, but..."

Usually means "I'm quite sure you're wrong." Not always hostile; some people use it to soften disagreement. Reply to the claim, not the framing.

12. The selective reply

You ask three questions; they answer the two easy ones and ignore the one that matters. Or they reply to everyone's messages in the channel except yours. This is harder to see than a phrase, and when it is deliberate it is more damaging. Reply by re-asking the one question, alone, in a new message.

Passive-aggressive, curt or bullying?

These are different problems and need different responses.

  • Curt: short, direct, no softening. Often a cultural or personal style, or a busy week. Feels cold; is not aimed at you.
  • Passive-aggressive: irritation expressed indirectly. Usually aimed at you, but often episodic and tied to a specific frustration that could be addressed directly.
  • Bullying: a sustained pattern of hostile behaviour aimed at one person, with an imbalance of power. Repeated public correction, exclusion, impossible deadlines and constant undermining over months are not a tone problem. Our guide to workplace bullying signs covers this, and if it fits, document the messages and speak to HR, a union representative or an employment adviser.

Andersson and Pearson (1999) described how low-level workplace incivility can spiral: one person's rudeness provokes another's, until something minor becomes a real conflict. Passive-aggressive messages are classic fuel for that spiral. The best time to break it is early, and directly.

A simple rule for replying to any of them

Answer the content, ignore the tone, then decide whether the pattern needs a conversation. Matching passive aggression with passive aggression always escalates. Calling it out in writing ("I find your tone unprofessional") usually does too. If the same person keeps sending these, a short, private, spoken conversation works better than any email: "I sometimes get the sense you're frustrated with me in our threads. Is there something I could do differently?"

Are you sending them yourself?

Most of us do, sometimes. A few rewrites that keep the point without the edge:

  • "Per my last email" → "The details are in my email from Tuesday; here they are again."
  • "Friendly reminder" → "Could you send this by Friday? It's holding up the report."
  • "Going forward, please..." → "Could we do X from now on? It would help with Y."
  • "K." → "OK, thanks, that works."

On emoji: one study found that smiley faces in first work emails to strangers lowered impressions of competence without increasing warmth (Glikson, Cheshin and van Kleef, 2018). Between colleagues who already know each other, the picture is more relaxed. Our guide to what your emoji use says has more.

Reading one person over time

The fastest way to know whether a colleague or manager is passive-aggressive, curt, or simply busy is to look at a long stretch of their messages instead of the one that stung. Is the curtness constant or new? Is it with everyone or with you? Does it follow particular topics? Frequently asked questions

What does "per my last email" really mean?

Usually it means the information was already sent and the sender is irritated it was missed. Sometimes it is just a formal way of pointing to earlier details. The best reply answers the substance: acknowledge it and move the task forward.

Is a thumbs-up reaction passive-aggressive?

Not necessarily. For many people it simply means read and agreed. It can feel dismissive when it is the only response to something that clearly needed a reply. If you need a real answer, ask a direct follow-up question.

How do I respond to a passive-aggressive email at work?

Answer the content, ignore the tone, and keep it brief. If the pattern repeats with the same person, raise it privately and in person rather than in writing.

What is the difference between passive aggression and workplace bullying?

Passive aggression is indirect irritation, often episodic and tied to a specific frustration. Bullying is a sustained pattern of hostile behaviour aimed at one person, usually with a power imbalance. Bullying calls for documentation and formal support, not just better phrasing.

Why do work messages sound ruder than they are meant to?

Text removes tone of voice and facial cues. Research shows senders overestimate how clearly their tone comes across, and recipients tend to read neutral work messages more negatively than intended.
