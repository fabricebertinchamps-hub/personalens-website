How many messages do you need to analyse someone's personality?

There is no single magic number, but there are useful thresholds. Around 100 messages from a person supports only a broad sketch. From about 300 you can see the main patterns. Reliable readings usually need several hundred to a few thousand messages, spread over weeks or months and covering different situations, including at least some disagreement. Even then, inferring personality from text is a modest-accuracy science, and any honest tool should say how confident it is.

Why the number matters at all

Every message is a tiny sample of behaviour. Any single sample is noisy: you were tired, they were in a meeting, autocorrect chose the word. Psychologists have known for decades that single observations predict poorly and averages predict well. Seymour Epstein's classic work on the aggregation principle (1979) showed that behaviour measured once looks inconsistent, while the same behaviour averaged over many occasions becomes stable and predictive.

William Fleeson (2001) made the same point from the other direction. Tracking people's behaviour several times a day, he found that everyone varies enormously from moment to moment. A highly extraverted person has plenty of quiet, withdrawn moments. What distinguishes them is the distribution of their behaviour over time, not any one instance. A personality, in this view, is a shape that only appears once you have enough points.

A chat is the same. Ten messages are ten points. You cannot see a shape in ten points.

What the research on language and personality says

Language does carry personality information

Pennebaker and King (1999) showed that people's word use is stable over time and relates to their personality. Later, large studies of social media text, notably Schwartz et al. (2013) and Park et al. (2015), predicted Big Five traits from the language of tens of thousands of people. These studies generally worked with substantial amounts of text per person, on the order of a thousand words or more, not a handful of posts.

Accuracy rises with data, then levels off

The clearest illustration of "more data, better judgement" comes from a related study. Youyou, Kosinski and Stillwell (2015, PNAS) compared computer predictions of personality from Facebook Likes with judgements by people who knew the participants. With about 10 Likes, the computer matched a work colleague; about 70 matched a friend or roommate; about 150 matched a family member; and about 300 matched a spouse. Likes are not messages, but the pattern generalises: accuracy climbs steeply with data at first, and each additional chunk adds less.

Even with lots of data, accuracy is modest

A meta-analysis of studies predicting the Big Five from digital footprints (Azucar, Marengo and Settanni, 2018) found correlations with self-reported personality mostly in the range of about .3 to .4. That is meaningful, and comparable to many judgements people make about each other, but far from a perfect read. Anyone promising certainty from a chat is overselling.

It is not just how many messages, but which ones

David Funder's Realistic Accuracy Model (1995) explains why. For anyone, person or software, to judge a trait accurately, four things have to happen: the person must do something relevant to the trait, it must be available to the observer, the observer must detect it, and then use it correctly. A thousand messages about who is picking up the kids are available but mostly not relevant to how someone handles intimacy.

Count words, not just messages

"ok", "👍" and "lol" are messages. They tell you something about tempo and engagement, but very little about how someone thinks. Two hundred long messages may say more than a thousand one-word replies.

Count each person separately

A 600-message chat where you wrote 500 of them contains only 100 messages from the other person. Most tools that report a total are quietly overstating how much they know about the quieter person.

Span matters more than volume

A thousand messages sent in one intense evening describe that evening. Three hundred messages over four months describe a relationship. Spread over time catches the variation Fleeson described, and lets you see change, which is often the real question.

You need some friction

This matters most for attachment. The attachment system activates under threat: distance, conflict, uncertainty. A chat that is entirely logistics and jokes shows you how two people coordinate, not how they handle needing each other. A few disagreements, a cancelled plan, a difficult piece of news: these are where anxious, avoidant and secure patterns actually show up. Our guide to attachment style from texts describes what to look for.

Some traits are easier to see than others

Simine Vazire's research (2010) found that other people judge highly visible traits, such as how talkative or outgoing someone is, better than inner states like anxiety, where people's own reports are more accurate. Text behaves similarly. Extraversion-related signals (how much someone writes, how often they initiate, how much they use social words) appear quickly. Emotional stability, which is largely internal, needs more data and more varied situations.

Practical thresholds

Here are the confidence bands Persona Lens uses when it reads a chat. They are our working thresholds, informed by the research above and by testing, not numbers from a single study:

  • Under 100 messages: very low. Expect general observations. Fine for "what's the tone of this exchange?", not for personality.
  • 100 to 300: low. Broad patterns: who initiates, rough tempo, dominant tone.
  • 300 to 800: medium. The main patterns of the relationship start to hold, especially if the chat spans several weeks.
  • 800 to 2,000: high. Detailed, reasonably stable readings of both people.
  • Over 2,000: very high. Enough to see change over time and to separate habits from moods.

Group chats need more, because the total is split across several people. A 1,000-message group chat with eight members may give you only a few dozen messages from the quietest ones. See group chat dynamics for what a group thread can reveal even when individual counts are low.

What if you only have a short chat?

Short chats are still useful if you ask smaller questions.

  • Ask about the exchange, not the person. "What happened in this conversation?" is answerable from 30 messages. "What is she like?" is not.
  • Read your own side. You have your whole history of writing to compare against. The Self lens focuses on how you write in this chat.
  • Add context, not more text. Telling the tool that this is a new relationship, or that the chat covers a stressful week, helps it weigh what it sees.
  • Look at first replies. Opening messages and immediate responses are disproportionately revealing. Our guide to why first replies matter more than long ones explains why.

How to get the most out of a longer chat

  • Export the whole chat rather than a hand-picked slice. Choosing the worst moments biases any reading. Our export guide shows how.
  • Make sure names are clear, so each message is attributed to the right person.
  • Be suspicious of certainty. A good reading tells you which conclusions are strongly supported and which are guesses. If a tool does not, ask, or use another.

The honest bottom line

Messages are better evidence than personality quizzes in one important way: they show what someone actually does, not how they describe themselves. Our guide to the Big Five from real behaviour makes that case in more detail. But behaviour-based inference is still inference. The more messages, the more varied the situations and the longer the span, the more weight a reading can bear. With a small chat, keep the question small too.

This guide is informational, not clinical. A reading of messages is an interpretation, not a psychological assessment or diagnosis.

Frequently asked questions

What is the minimum number of messages to analyse personality?

Around 100 messages from the person allows a broad sketch, about 300 gives a usable reading of the main patterns, and several hundred to a few thousand spread over time gives a reliable one. Fewer messages can still describe a single exchange but not a personality.

Can you tell someone's attachment style from a few texts?

Rarely. Attachment patterns show up mainly under stress, such as conflict, distance or uncertainty. A short or purely logistical chat usually lacks those moments, so it says little about attachment however many messages it contains.

How accurate is personality analysis from text?

Moderately. Research predicting the Big Five from digital text typically finds correlations with self-reported personality of roughly .3 to .4. That is meaningful but far from certain, so good tools state their confidence and show their evidence.

Do group chats need more messages?

Yes. The total is shared across several people, so each member may have contributed only a small share. Check how many messages each person wrote, not just the total.

Is it better to analyse a long chat or a recent one?

Usually a long one that spans weeks or months, because it captures how someone behaves across different moods and situations. If your question is about a recent change, a long chat also lets you compare before and after.
