The silent treatment: what it means and how to respond

The silent treatment is when someone deliberately stops talking to you, or replies only in clipped, minimal ways, to show displeasure, punish you or pressure you to change, while refusing to say what is wrong. It is different from needing space, which is announced and has an end, and from stonewalling, which is shutting down in the middle of a conflict because of overwhelm. Used repeatedly to control someone, it is a recognised form of emotional abuse, and research on ostracism shows that even short periods of being ignored reliably hurt.

Silent treatment vs. needing space vs. stonewalling

The three look identical from the outside: someone is not talking to you. They are different on the inside, and the right response depends on which one you are dealing with.

Silent treatment (also called the silence treatment, or punitive silence): a deliberate refusal to communicate, used as a message. The silence is meant to be felt. The person usually will not say what is wrong, and the silence ends when you apologise, give in, or when they decide it is over.

Needing space: a request for time to calm down or think, said out loud and usually time-limited. "I need tonight to think, can we talk tomorrow?" is needing space. The person comes back on their own and engages with the issue.

Stonewalling: the term the psychologist John Gottman uses for withdrawing from an argument, going blank, looking away, giving no responses. In his research it most often follows emotional flooding, a state of physiological overload in which the person genuinely cannot process more input. It is a shutdown, not necessarily a strategy, though it still damages the relationship over time.

Signs it is the silent treatment, not just a quiet patch

  • The silence started right after a disagreement or after you said no to something.
  • They talk normally to everyone else, sometimes conspicuously so, while you get nothing or one-word replies.
  • You ask what is wrong and hear "nothing" or "you know what you did".
  • Your messages show as read, but there is no reply, for hours or days.
  • It ends only when you apologise, often for something you are not sure you did.
  • It happens repeatedly, around the same kinds of topics.
  • There is no conversation about it afterwards; things just "go back to normal" until the next time.
  • You start changing what you say in advance to avoid triggering it.

One or two of these after a bad argument can be ordinary. Several of them, as a pattern, is the silent treatment.

Why people use it

Most people who go silent are not plotting. In practice there are three common drivers, and they can overlap.

1. Emotional flooding

The person is overwhelmed and their capacity to talk has shut down. It usually follows a conflict spike, they look exhausted rather than cold, and they often come back wanting to repair. This overlaps with Gottman's stonewalling.

2. Avoidant coping

People with a more avoidant attachment style tend to withdraw under emotional pressure, because distance is how they learned to regulate. The silence is predictable around emotional topics, it is not escalated or weaponised, and they do not seem surprised when you raise it later. Our guide to why partners shut down during arguments goes deeper on this.

3. Deliberate punishment or control

The silence is calculated. The person knows it hurts and uses it to get a change in your behaviour. It is selective (around topics they want to control), it ends when you comply, and there is no remorse afterwards. This is the version that belongs in the coercive-control literature.

What the research says

Being ignored hurts in a measurable way. The social psychologist Kipling Williams has studied ostracism, being ignored and excluded, for more than two decades. His book Ostracism: The Power of Silence (Williams, 2001) describes the silent treatment as one of its most common forms in close relationships. In the Cyberball experiments (Williams, Cheung & Choi, 2000), people who were simply left out of an online ball-tossing game by strangers reported lower feelings of belonging, self-esteem, control and meaningful existence. If a few minutes of exclusion by strangers does that, days of silence from a partner or parent is not a small thing.

It overlaps with how the brain processes pain. In an fMRI study, Eisenberger, Lieberman and Williams (2003, Science) found that being excluded activated the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, a region also involved in the distress of physical pain. "It hurts" is not just a figure of speech.

Stonewalling predicts relationship breakdown. In John Gottman's long-term studies of married couples (Gottman, 1994), stonewalling was one of the "Four Horsemen", alongside criticism, contempt and defensiveness, the four communication patterns that best predicted later divorce.

The demand/withdraw loop makes it worse for both people. Researchers including Andrew Christensen (Christensen & Heavey, 1990) described the demand/withdraw pattern: one partner pushes for discussion, the other withdraws, which makes the first push harder, which makes the second withdraw further. A meta-analysis by Schrodt, Witt and Shimkowski (2014, Communication Monographs) pooled the research on this pattern and found it consistently associated with lower relationship satisfaction and poorer communication, and with individual distress for the people involved. Silence is rarely a one-person problem; it is usually half of a loop.

When it is used to control, it is part of abuse. The sociologist Evan Stark's work on coercive control (Stark, 2007) describes how abuse often works through patterns of intimidation, isolation and control rather than single incidents. Withholding communication to punish and regulate a partner's behaviour fits that pattern, which is why many domestic abuse organisations list the silent treatment as a form of emotional abuse.

What you can see in the chat history

The silent treatment is unusually visible in text, because the absence of messages leaves a trace. When you scroll back through months of a WhatsApp or Telegram conversation rather than looking at one bad week, a withdrawal pattern tends to show up in a few concrete ways: reply times that jump from minutes to a day after specific kinds of disagreement, messages that shrink from paragraphs to "ok" and "fine", your messages piling up unanswered in a block, the same person always being the one who breaks the silence, and the silence ending with an apology from you rather than a conversation about what happened. Seeing the whole timeline also helps you tell the versions apart: flooding tends to produce short, quickly repaired gaps, while punitive silence produces longer gaps that line up with topics and end on compliance. You can do this by hand by exporting the chat (see how to export a WhatsApp chat), or run it through Persona Lens, which reads the actual message history and points to the specific lines and gaps behind each observation. What to say and do: example messages

If someone is giving you the silent treatment

The instinct is to chase: more messages, apologies for things you are not sure you did, attempts to manufacture normality. That usually deepens the pattern. If the silence is flooding, chasing adds input they cannot process; if it is avoidant, it confirms that closeness is the threat; if it is punishment, it rewards the tactic. Instead, name it once, stay available, and stop.

  • "I've noticed you've gone quiet since yesterday. I'm here when you're ready to talk about it."
  • "I can tell something upset you. I'd rather hear it than guess. Can we talk tonight or tomorrow?"
  • "I'm not going to keep messaging, but I'm not ignoring this either. When you want to talk, tell me."
  • For a repeated pattern: "When you stop talking to me for days after a disagreement, I feel shut out and I end up apologising just to make it stop. I want us to find a different way to handle conflict."

What not to send: a wall of messages, a matched silence of your own, or an apology you do not mean. Matching their silence looks proportionate but escalates the loop.

If you are the one who goes silent

The fix is a bridge message: one line that turns silence into a request for space.

  • "I'm too wound up to talk well right now. I need a couple of hours, and I'll come back to this tonight."
  • "I'm not ignoring you. I need to think before I reply. Can we talk tomorrow morning?"

The Gottman Institute advises couples who feel flooded to take a break of at least 20 minutes and do something calming before returning. The key word is returning: a break with a stated time and a real comeback is needing space; a break with no end is the silent treatment.

How long is too long?

Hours: usually flooding or processing. Most adults need some space after a hard moment.

A day or two: avoidant territory if it is a habit, but still within normal patterns for many people, especially if they come back and talk.

Several days, repeatedly: the silence is now functioning as control, whatever the original intent. A pattern like this is corrosive even when neither person set out to make it so.

When it is abuse, and where to get help

The silent treatment crosses into emotional abuse when it is repeated, deliberate and used to punish or control you, for example when it lasts days, ends only when you apologise or comply, and leaves you changing your behaviour out of fear of the next silence. It is a stronger warning sign when it comes with other controlling behaviour: checking your phone, controlling money, cutting you off from friends or family, threats, or intimidation. Our guide to emotional abuse patterns in relationships covers these in detail.

If this sounds like your situation, you do not have to sort it out alone:

  • United States: National Domestic Violence Hotline, call 1-800-799-7233 or text START to 88788 (thehotline.org).
  • United Kingdom: National Domestic Abuse Helpline (run by Refuge), 0808 2000 247, free and open 24 hours.
  • Elsewhere: contact your local domestic abuse service, a GP or a counsellor. If you are in immediate danger, call your local emergency number.

The repair conversation

Once the silence breaks, the temptation is to skip past it out of relief. Don't. The conversation about the silence matters more than the original disagreement. A useful opener: "I want to understand what was going on for you when you went quiet. Were you overwhelmed, hurt, avoiding it, or angry with me? Knowing helps me respond better next time." An honest answer, even an uncomfortable one, is the start of a different pattern.

This guide is informational, not clinical. If you are in distress or a relationship feels unsafe, please reach out to a qualified professional or a local support service.

Frequently asked questions

What is the silent treatment?

The silent treatment is a deliberate refusal to communicate with someone, or replying only in minimal ways, to show displeasure, punish them or pressure them to change, without saying what is wrong. It differs from needing space, which is announced and time-limited.

Is the silent treatment a form of emotional abuse?

When it is used deliberately and repeatedly to punish or control someone, yes; many domestic abuse organisations list it as emotional abuse and it fits the pattern researchers call coercive control. When it comes from flooding or avoidant coping, it is a communication breakdown rather than abuse, though it still damages the relationship over time.

What is the difference between the silent treatment and stonewalling?

Stonewalling, a term from John Gottman's research, is shutting down during a conflict, usually because the person is emotionally flooded. The silent treatment is a longer, deliberate silence used as a message or punishment. Stonewalling is often a shutdown; the silent treatment is usually a strategy.

How should I respond to the silent treatment?

Name it once, calmly, and say you are available to talk, for example: "I've noticed you've gone quiet. I'm here when you're ready to talk." Then stop messaging and get on with your day. Avoid chasing, apologising for things you did not do, or matching the silence.

Should I match their silence?

No. Matching looks proportionate but escalates the pattern into a standoff. Name it, stay available, and do not chase.

How do I stop giving people the silent treatment myself?

Use a bridge message you can send even when you cannot talk yet, such as: "I'm overwhelmed and need a few hours. I'll come back to this tonight." A stated break with a real return is needing space, not punishment.

Can you see the silent treatment in text messages?

Often, yes. Over a long chat history it shows up as reply times that jump after certain disagreements, replies shrinking to one word, your messages piling up unanswered, and silences that end with your apology rather than a conversation.
